# Benchmark results

All runs happened on 2026-10-19 on the host described in [BENCHMARK_PLAN.md §2](BENCHMARK_PLAN.md#2-test-environment).
Every number here comes from [`results/raw/*.jsonl`](results/raw/) (one JSON record per run, with the full
configuration, per-process CPU, RSS and Valkey INFO deltas). The tables are generated by `bench/summarize.py`
into [`results/key_tables.md`](results/key_tables.md) and [`results/summary.md`](results/summary.md), which has
full per-scenario tables including CV, p95, CPU and cost columns. Binary hashes and versions are in
[`results/environment.json`](results/environment.json).

**Conventions.** Throughput is the median of 3 independent trials, each in fresh processes, with the
[min–max] range where shown. Latency percentiles are medians across trials of each trial's client-side
percentile. "Server CPU µs/op" is the CPU of the whole data-service side (Valkey processes plus the proxy, if
any) divided by operations; it equals CPU-seconds per million operations. "Best native" means the better of
`valkey-io2` and `valkey-2shard`, the Valkey-only uses of the same 2 vCPUs.

## 1. Findings

1. **Valkey's CPU goes mostly to the kernel.** On every core workload, single-threaded Valkey spent
   64–71% (median 69%) of its CPU in the kernel (§8). Executing `GET` itself (`INFO commandstats`, profiling
   run) took 1.85 µs, against 11.8 µs of total CPU per operation on `read90`.
2. **The Rust proxy lost to the Valkey-native use of the same 2 vCPUs in every scenario where both ran,
   except connection churn,** where all topologies were limited by the client and the proxy's CPU per operation
   (121 µs) fell between `valkey-1t` (72) and `valkey-io2` (141). At 50 connections it
   reached 0.60–0.67× the throughput of two Valkey shards, at 1.36–1.51× the CPU per operation (K1). The gap
   narrows with more connections (0.76× at 1000) but never closes (K2). With 16 KiB values the proxy was worse
   than single-threaded Valkey: 0.69× its throughput and 2.4× the CPU per operation of the shards (K4).
3. **The proxy adds latency.** At 1 connection, p50 rose from 63 µs to 150 µs. At every fixed offered load it
   had the highest p50 and p99 of all topologies: p50 2.2–2.3× and p99 1.5–2.0× the best native topology. Only
   `valkey-io2`'s p99.9 was worse, at 40k and 60k (K3).
4. **The proxy's mechanism works, but moves cost instead of removing it.** Behind the proxy, Valkey's CPU per
   operation dropped to 3.2–4.1 µs, because it received batches of 13 commands per write at 50
   connections and 56 at 1000. The proxy then spent 15.3–17.0 µs per operation, 70–72% of it in the
   kernel, on the same per-request system calls Valkey had been making (§8).
5. **Batching in the client beats everything else measured.** Pipelining at depth 16 raised single-threaded
   Valkey from 83,603 to 479,265 ops/s (5.7×) at 2.0 µs/op. Two shards with pipelining reached 996,709 ops/s.
   Adding the proxy to already-pipelined clients gained nothing (479,646 ops/s on 1.55 vCPUs) (K4).
6. **`io-threads 2` is counterproductive on a 2-vCPU budget.** It gave 0.82–0.85× single-threaded throughput at
   2.3–2.4× the CPU per operation, and burned 155% CPU at a load of 13k ops/s (K1, K2). This matches Valkey's
   guidance to enable it on 3–4+ cores. It is not evidence against `io-threads` on larger hosts.
7. **AOF and connection churn**: with AOF on, the proxy beat single-threaded Valkey (1.17–1.24×), but two shards with AOF
   reached 1.5–1.8× the proxy, and client pipelining with AOF reached 190k ops/s on writes. With a new connection
   per request, all topologies were limited to ~12–13k ops/s by client connection setup; the proxy used 1.7× the
   server-side CPU per operation of plain Valkey. Persistent connections alone gave 6.4× (§7).
8. **No measured correctness problems in any throughput run:** zero errors and timeouts, zero reply
   misattribution, and client- and server-measured throughput within 1.3% (§2).

## 2. Validity of the measurements

- loadgen runs checked: 198
- runs where a GET received a non-bulk, non-nil reply (reply misattribution): 0
- runs flagged client-bound (load generator > 170% CPU): 3
- client vs Valkey-side (INFO) throughput, absolute difference: median 0.2%, max 1.3% (n=186; open-loop and pipelined runs excluded)
- every throughput run had zero RESP errors, zero timeouts and zero lost connections
- the binaries' SHA-256 hashes matched `results/environment.json` after the last run

### Load generator cross-check (GET only, `valkey-1t`, 50 connections)

|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

The two load generators agree within their trial ranges. `loadgen` used less client CPU (94% vs 135%), so it is
not the bottleneck where `valkey-benchmark` is not.

### Noise

Across the 58 closed-loop points, the throughput CV between trials had a median of 3.6%, a 90th percentile
of 6.0%, and a maximum of 10.1% (`valkey-1t` `write90-aof`); see `summary.md`. Every difference reported as a
finding is larger than the min–max ranges of the configurations compared.

### Runs that are not data points

* `valkey-2shard` at 1 connection (3 runs): not applicable. One connection cannot reach two shards, and
  `loadgen` refuses the configuration.
* `valkey-2shard` on `skew-read90` (3 runs) was flagged client-bound (load generator at 176% CPU), so its
  throughput is a **lower bound**. This understates the native topology's advantage over the proxy.

| 40,000 | 322 / 3,391 / 12,894 (13.0) | 246 / 3,539 / 25,903 (35.6) | 201 / 2,773 / 7,668 (14.8) | 441 / 4,088 / 13,287 (21.2) |

* Coordinated omission check: open-loop schedule lag is recorded per run (`sched_lag_us` in the raw data). Sends

* `valkey-io2` has a low p50 but the worst p99.9 at 40k and 60k, and the highest CPU per operation at every load,

Notes:

* **Large values:** every byte crosses two hops through the proxy (32,861 loopback bytes per op against 16,430
  direct) and is copied in user space. The proxy's throughput fell below single-threaded Valkey.
* **TTL:** hit rates differ by topology because faster topologies write more keys in each 5 s TTL window. Valkey
  expired 0.46–0.79M keys per run with no errors. The proxy does not change expiry semantics because it holds no
  data.
* **Cold cache:** the miss path costs about the same as the hit path in every topology.
* **Eviction (Valkey-only characterization):** steady-state `allkeys-lru` under 256 MB gave a 0.322 hit rate for
  uniform access, about the cached fraction of the 5M-key space, and 0.699 under Zipf 0.99. Throughput dropped
  only to 66–69k ops/s from 74–84k without eviction. This is the baseline a Rust sidecar cache's eviction would
  have to beat (Option B).
* **AOF and churn:** with `appendonly yes`, `everysec`, the proxy beat single-threaded Valkey (`read90` 1.17×,
  `write90` 1.24×). Its batching cuts Valkey's per-iteration AOF work, and `valkey-1t` was only 75% CPU-busy on
  `write90-aof`. But two shards with AOF reached 135,143 and 106,744 ops/s (proxy at 0.65× and 0.55×), and
  client pipelining on one Valkey with AOF reached 190,328 ops/s on `write90` at 3.4 µs/op. Under connection
  churn (`-k 0`) all three topologies were held to 12.0–12.9k ops/s by connection setup in the client, with no
  server CPU saturated. The proxy used 121 µs of data-service CPU per operation against 72 µs for `valkey-1t`.
  Persistent connections on the same tool gave 76.6k ops/s at 12.6 µs/op (cross-check table, §2).

Notes on the tables above:

* **Memory:** Valkey's per-key overhead (53.5 bytes above the 116-byte payload) is half that of a naive Rust

Workload `read90` at 50 connections (K1). The price is an **assumed illustrative parameter** ($0.05 per vCPU-hour),
not a quote. `bench/summarize.py --vcpu-hour X` recomputes every cost column. "Throughput per provisioned vCPU"
is the measured throughput divided by the vCPUs the topology occupies. `valkey-1t` occupies only 1 vCPU, because
it cannot use a second one.

| topology | consumed CPU-s per Mop | throughput per provisioned vCPU | vCPUs for 1M ops/s (provisioned) | monthly cost at $0.05/vCPU-h, 730 h | vs `valkey-2shard` |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| valkey-1t | 11.8 | 83,603 ops/s | 12.0 | $437 | −$126 (0.78×) |

## 13. Limitations and measurements that remain unverified

Measured here but **specific to this host**:

* Absolute throughput and latency (KVM guest, loopback, 4 vCPUs). The kernel's share of CPU is likely higher
  than on bare metal, which **favors** batching architectures such as the proxy, so the proxy's deficit here is
  if anything understated.

**Not measured** (no infrastructure, or the repository lacks the input):

* The production workload, and any production bottleneck (ASSESSMENT.md §2).
* Real network RTT effects (no `netem`). Discussed only as a model, ASSESSMENT.md §7.
* TLS cost (the proxy has no TLS; TLS on both hops would add CPU on the proxy path).
* `io-threads` on hosts with 4 or more cores for the data service.
* Real cluster mode (slot routing, `MOVED`, cluster bus) as opposed to client-routed shards; hot-key load
  imbalance between shards.
* Replication, failover with replicas, network partitions.
