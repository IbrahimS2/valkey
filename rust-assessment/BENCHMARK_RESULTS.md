# Benchmark results

This file has two parts. **Part I** (§1–13) is the original assessment of a Rust proxy. **Part II** (from
"Part II: reassessment results" below) measures a Rust-native server and established Rust client
functionality. The two parts ran on **different physical hosts** (REASSESSMENT.md §2), so their absolute
numbers must not be compared. Each part compares configurations measured in the same session only.

# Part I: proxy assessment

All runs happened on 2026-10-19 on the host described in [BENCHMARK_PLAN.md §2](BENCHMARK_PLAN.md#2-test-environment).
Every number here comes from [`results/raw/*.jsonl`](results/raw/) (one JSON record per run, with the full
configuration, per-process CPU, RSS and Valkey INFO deltas). The tables are generated by `bench/summarize.py`
into [`results/key_tables.md`](results/key_tables.md) and [`results/summary.md`](results/summary.md), which has
full per-scenario tables including CV, p95, CPU and cost columns. Binary hashes and versions are in
[`results/environment.json`](results/environment.json).

**Conventions.** Throughput is the median of 3 independent trials, each in fresh processes, with the
[min–max] range where shown. Latency percentiles are medians across trials of each trial's client-side
percentile. "Server CPU µs/op" is the CPU of the whole data-service side (Valkey processes plus the proxy, if
any) divided by operations; it equals CPU-seconds per million operations. "Best native" means the better of
`valkey-io2` and `valkey-2shard`, the Valkey-only uses of the same 2 vCPUs.

## 1. Findings

1. **Valkey's CPU goes mostly to the kernel.** On every core workload, single-threaded Valkey spent
   64–71% (median 69%) of its CPU in the kernel (§8). Executing `GET` itself (`INFO commandstats`, profiling
   run) took 1.85 µs, against 11.8 µs of total CPU per operation on `read90`.
2. **The Rust proxy lost to the Valkey-native use of the same 2 vCPUs in every scenario where both ran,
   except connection churn,** where all topologies were limited by the client and the proxy's CPU per operation
   (121 µs) fell between `valkey-1t` (72) and `valkey-io2` (141). At 50 connections it
   reached 0.60–0.67× the throughput of two Valkey shards, at 1.36–1.51× the CPU per operation (K1). The gap
   narrows with more connections (0.76× at 1000) but never closes (K2). With 16 KiB values the proxy was worse
   than single-threaded Valkey: 0.69× its throughput and 2.4× the CPU per operation of the shards (K4).
3. **The proxy adds latency.** At 1 connection, p50 rose from 63 µs to 150 µs. At every fixed offered load it
   had the highest p50 and p99 of all topologies: p50 2.2–2.3× and p99 1.5–2.0× the best native topology. Only
   `valkey-io2`'s p99.9 was worse, at 40k and 60k (K3).
4. **The proxy's mechanism works, but moves cost instead of removing it.** Behind the proxy, Valkey's CPU per
   operation dropped to 3.2–4.1 µs, because it received batches of 13 commands per write at 50
   connections and 56 at 1000. The proxy then spent 15.3–17.0 µs per operation, 70–72% of it in the
   kernel, on the same per-request system calls Valkey had been making (§8).
5. **Batching in the client beats everything else measured.** Pipelining at depth 16 raised single-threaded
   Valkey from 83,603 to 479,265 ops/s (5.7×) at 2.0 µs/op. Two shards with pipelining reached 996,709 ops/s.
   Adding the proxy to already-pipelined clients gained nothing (479,646 ops/s on 1.55 vCPUs) (K4).
6. **`io-threads 2` is counterproductive on a 2-vCPU budget.** It gave 0.82–0.85× single-threaded throughput at
   2.3–2.4× the CPU per operation, and burned 155% CPU at a load of 13k ops/s (K1, K2). This matches Valkey's
   guidance to enable it on 3–4+ cores. It is not evidence against `io-threads` on larger hosts.
7. **AOF and connection churn**: with AOF on, the proxy beat single-threaded Valkey (1.17–1.24×), but two shards with AOF
   reached 1.5–1.8× the proxy, and client pipelining with AOF reached 190k ops/s on writes. With a new connection
   per request, all topologies were limited to ~12–13k ops/s by client connection setup; the proxy used 1.7× the
   server-side CPU per operation of plain Valkey. Persistent connections alone gave 6.4× (§7).
8. **No measured correctness problems in any throughput run:** zero errors and timeouts, zero reply
   misattribution, and client- and server-measured throughput within 1.3% (§2).

## 2. Validity of the measurements

- loadgen runs checked: 198
- runs where a GET received a non-bulk, non-nil reply (reply misattribution): 0
- runs flagged client-bound (load generator > 170% CPU): 3
- client vs Valkey-side (INFO) throughput, absolute difference: median 0.2%, max 1.3% (n=186; open-loop and pipelined runs excluded)
- every throughput run had zero RESP errors, zero timeouts and zero lost connections
- the binaries' SHA-256 hashes matched `results/environment.json` after the last run

### Load generator cross-check (GET only, `valkey-1t`, 50 connections)

| tool | n | ops/s median [min–max] | CV | p50 µs | p99 µs | p99.9 µs | server CPU % | client CPU % |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| valkey-benchmark | 3 | 76,610 [70,262–80,367] | 6.7% | 503 | 1,847 | 4,743 | 97 | 135 |

The two load generators agree within their trial ranges. `loadgen` used less client CPU (94% vs 135%), so it is
not the bottleneck where `valkey-benchmark` is not.

### Noise

Across the 58 closed-loop points, the throughput CV between trials had a median of 3.6%, a 90th percentile
of 6.0%, and a maximum of 10.1% (`valkey-1t` `write90-aof`); see `summary.md`. Every difference reported as a
finding is larger than the min–max ranges of the configurations compared.

### Runs that are not data points

* `valkey-2shard` at 1 connection (3 runs): not applicable. One connection cannot reach two shards, and
  `loadgen` refuses the configuration.
* `valkey-2shard` on `skew-read90` (3 runs) was flagged client-bound (load generator at 176% CPU), so its
  throughput is a **lower bound**. This understates the native topology's advantage over the proxy.

## 6. Latency at a fixed offered load (open loop)

p50 / p99 / p99.9 µs (server CPU µs/op).

| offered ops/s | valkey-1t | valkey-io2 | valkey-2shard | rust-proxy |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 20,000 | 239 / 1,991 / 5,202 (19.2) | 181 / 2,593 / 8,610 (65.8) | 157 / 2,033 / 7,016 (22.4) | 361 / 3,611 / 9,093 (34.0) |
| 40,000 | 322 / 3,391 / 12,894 (13.0) | 246 / 3,539 / 25,903 (35.6) | 201 / 2,773 / 7,668 (14.8) | 441 / 4,088 / 13,287 (21.2) |
| 60,000 | 361 / 4,321 / 10,830 (9.2) | 273 / 5,079 / 14,860 (24.6) | 224 / 2,568 / 7,414 (10.5) | 484 / 5,235 / 14,213 (15.6) |

* Coordinated omission check: open-loop schedule lag is recorded per run (`sched_lag_us` in the raw data). Sends
  are paced by tokio's 1 ms timer, so all topologies see the same bursty arrivals. That burstiness is why p99
  values are in the milliseconds even at 20k ops/s.
* At every load the proxy had the highest p50, 2.2–2.3× that of the best native topology (two shards) and
  1.3–1.5× single-threaded Valkey's. Its p99 was 1.5–2.0× the best native p99, which fails acceptance
  criterion 2 (≤ 1.10×).
* `valkey-io2` has a low p50 but the worst p99.9 at 40k and 60k, and the highest CPU per operation at every load,
  because its I/O thread busy-polls.

Notes:

* **Large values:** every byte crosses two hops through the proxy (32,861 loopback bytes per op against 16,430
  direct) and is copied in user space. The proxy's throughput fell below single-threaded Valkey.
* **TTL:** hit rates differ by topology because faster topologies write more keys in each 5 s TTL window. Valkey
  expired 0.46–0.79M keys per run with no errors. The proxy does not change expiry semantics because it holds no
  data.
* **Cold cache:** the miss path costs about the same as the hit path in every topology.
* **Eviction (Valkey-only characterization):** steady-state `allkeys-lru` under 256 MB gave a 0.322 hit rate for
  uniform access, about the cached fraction of the 5M-key space, and 0.699 under Zipf 0.99. Throughput dropped
  only to 66–69k ops/s from 74–84k without eviction. This is the baseline a Rust sidecar cache's eviction would
  have to beat (Option B).
* **AOF and churn:** with `appendonly yes`, `everysec`, the proxy beat single-threaded Valkey (`read90` 1.17×,
  `write90` 1.24×). Its batching cuts Valkey's per-iteration AOF work, and `valkey-1t` was only 75% CPU-busy on
  `write90-aof`. But two shards with AOF reached 135,143 and 106,744 ops/s (proxy at 0.65× and 0.55×), and
  client pipelining on one Valkey with AOF reached 190,328 ops/s on `write90` at 3.4 µs/op. Under connection
  churn (`-k 0`) all three topologies were held to 12.0–12.9k ops/s by connection setup in the client, with no
  server CPU saturated. The proxy used 121 µs of data-service CPU per operation against 72 µs for `valkey-1t`.
  Persistent connections on the same tool gave 76.6k ops/s at 12.6 µs/op (cross-check table, §2).

Notes on the tables above:

* **Memory:** Valkey's per-key overhead (53.5 bytes above the 116-byte payload) is half that of a naive Rust
  map (114 bytes). That map has no TTL index, eviction metadata or concurrency. With 16 KiB values, Valkey pays
  jemalloc's 20 KiB size class (+25%), an allocator effect a Rust service using jemalloc would share.
* **Failover:** through the proxy, 0, 16 and 5 clients in the three trials never saw the backend die. They
  continued against the empty restarted Valkey without an error, which is why their hit rates fell. Direct
  clients all saw the disconnect. See THREAT_MODEL.md §6.2.

## 11. Cost per million operations

Workload `read90` at 50 connections (K1). The price is an **assumed illustrative parameter** ($0.05 per vCPU-hour),
not a quote. `bench/summarize.py --vcpu-hour X` recomputes every cost column. "Throughput per provisioned vCPU"
is the measured throughput divided by the vCPUs the topology occupies. `valkey-1t` occupies only 1 vCPU, because
it cannot use a second one.

| topology | consumed CPU-s per Mop | throughput per provisioned vCPU | vCPUs for 1M ops/s (provisioned) | monthly cost at $0.05/vCPU-h, 730 h | vs `valkey-2shard` |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| valkey-1t | 11.8 | 83,603 ops/s | 12.0 | $437 | −$126 (0.78×) |
| valkey-io2 | 28.5 | 34,379 ops/s | 29.1 | $1,062 | +$499 (1.89×) |
| valkey-2shard | 14.8 | 64,916 ops/s | 15.4 | $562 | +$0 (1.00×) |
| rust-proxy | 20.1 | 43,460 ops/s | 23.0 | $840 | +$278 (1.49×) |

The 1M ops/s column **assumes linear scale-out** of the measured unit. That assumption is not verified, and it is
optimistic for every topology alike. Per-scenario $/Bop (2 provisioned vCPUs) is in `summary.md`. The proxy's
consumed CPU per operation was 1.30–2.40× the shards' in every scenario where both ran (2.40× with 16 KiB
values). Under client-limited connection churn, where the shards were not run, it was 0.86× `valkey-io2`'s and
1.68× `valkey-1t`'s. No scenario produced a saving to set against development cost, so there is no break-even point.

## 13. Limitations and measurements that remain unverified

Measured here but **specific to this host**:

* Absolute throughput and latency (KVM guest, loopback, 4 vCPUs). The kernel's share of CPU is likely higher
  than on bare metal, which **favors** batching architectures such as the proxy, so the proxy's deficit here is
  if anything understated.

**Not measured** (no infrastructure, or the repository lacks the input):

* The production workload, and any production bottleneck (ASSESSMENT.md §2).
* Real network RTT effects (no `netem`). Discussed only as a model, ASSESSMENT.md §7.
* TLS cost (the proxy has no TLS; TLS on both hops would add CPU on the proxy path).
* `io-threads` on hosts with 4 or more cores for the data service.
* Real cluster mode (slot routing, `MOVED`, cluster bus) as opposed to client-routed shards; hot-key load
  imbalance between shards.
* Replication, failover with replicas, network partitions.
* Hours-long runs (fragmentation, rehashing, latency outliers over time).
* Independent replication by a second party or on a second machine. Reproduction steps are in

## II.1 Findings

1. **Per core, Rust brought no advantage.** Round 1's `rvk-1t` reached 0.80–0.91× of `valkey-1t` unpipelined
   and 0.73–0.96× at depth 16. After round 2's changes it was at parity: 0.97–1.03× unpipelined and
   0.96–1.14× at depth 16. Both the deficit and its removal came from implementation choices (§II.4), not the
   language.
2. **Multi-threading beats Valkey's single-endpoint option on 2 vCPUs, by a wide margin.** R2 was 2.4–2.8×
   unpipelined and 1.2–1.9× pipelined in round 1, and 2.8–3.2× and 1.5–2.9× in round 2. `io-threads 2` is weak
   on this budget (Part I), so this measures rvk against a configuration outside its intended regime.
3. **Against two Valkey shards on the same 2 CPUs, rvk-2t roughly matched them and never beat them by the
   required 20%.** Unpipelined, rvk-2t was level or ahead (R3 0.99–1.17 in round 1, 1.05–1.19 in round 2) at
   lower CPU per operation, but most of those points were limited by the load generator. Pipelined, which removes that
   limit, R3 was 0.76–0.88 in round 1 and 0.85–1.06 in round 2.
4. **Pre-registered Gate 3 (round 1): NO-GO.** 0 of 5 points met GO and 1 of 5 met CONDITIONAL GO (3 needed).
   **Round 2, post hoc:** 0 of 5 met GO. 4 of 5 met the CONDITIONAL GO throughput condition. The latency condition
   failed at the lowest offered load: at 20k ops/s, rvk-2t's p99 was 1.42× that of the best single-endpoint
   Valkey (1.58× in round 1).
5. **The round 2 speed-up cost memory.** Round 2's table layout raised `used_memory` per key from 172.5 to
   197.6 bytes (Valkey: 169.5). Under a 256 MB limit, rvk then held 1.0M keys against Valkey's 1.61M, and its
   uniform-access hit rate fell to 0.200 against Valkey's 0.322.
6. **Client-side multiplexing (Candidate C) beat every server change.** 50 logical clients on one socket with
   redis-rs raised single-threaded Valkey from 117,740 to 200,356 ops/s (1.70×), and cut its CPU per operation
   from 8.26 to 1.89 µs (−77%), with no server change. The experimental client-side cache added only 1.14× more
   and returned stale data on 34% of reads.
7. **The proxy (Candidate B) is still dominated.** On today's host it reached 132,926 ops/s at 13.18 µs/op,
   against 236,016 ops/s at 8.22 µs/op for two shards (0.56×, 1.60× CPU/op).
8. **No measured correctness problem in any run:** 0 error replies, 0 timeouts and 0 lost connections in 573
   load-generator and 42 client-library runs. No reply misattribution was visible. Client-side and server-side
   throughput agreed within 1.5%.

## II.4 Root causes (profiles, `bench/profile_part2.sh`)

`perf record -e cpu-clock -g` on the server process, GET-only, 50 connections. Use these for attribution; sampling
lowers throughput. Files: `results/profile2/` (round 1) and `results/profile2_r2/` (round 2).

| Profile | Kernel | Own code | libc | Top entries |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| valkey-1t, unpipelined | 72.1% | 23.3% | 4.6% | `_raw_spin_unlock_irqrestore` 18.7% (k), `hashtableObjectGetKey` 4.0%, `isCopyAvoidPreferred` 3.2%, `findBucket` 3.1% |
| rvk-1t round 1, unpipelined | 82.7% | 10.1% | 6.7% | `_raw_spin_unlock_irqrestore` **33.4%** (k), `Shard::lookup` 7.1%, `memcmp` 3.6% |
| valkey-1t, depth 16 | 29.6% | 52.0% | 18.5% | `memmove` 13.0%, `processInputBuffer` 6.1%, `_raw_spin_unlock_irqrestore` 5.1%, `getMonotonicUs` 5.0%, `call` 3.4%; key lookup about 6% |
| rvk-1t round 1, depth 16 | 44.6% | 37.7% | 15.6% | `Shard::lookup` **28.5%**, `_raw_spin_unlock_irqrestore` **23.6%** (k), `memcmp` **12.7%** |
| rvk-1t round 2, depth 16 | 29.9% | 40.5% | 27.4% | `Shard::lookup` 29.7%, `memcmp` 24.0%, `_raw_spin_unlock_irqrestore` 5.9% (k) |

* **Wakeups.** `_raw_spin_unlock_irqrestore` is where the kernel re-enables interrupts after waking a task, here
  the load-generator thread on another CPU. Round 1 wrote each connection's replies as soon as it produced
  them, so the client was woken repeatedly. Valkey writes all replies at the end of an event-loop pass
  (`beforeSleep`), so the client is woken once per pass. Round 2 does the same. At depth 16, that cost fell
  from 23.6% to 5.9%.
* **Lookups.** Each rvk lookup chases dependent cache misses (table, entry, key bytes). Valkey prefetches keys
  for a whole pipelined batch (`memory_prefetch.c`); its lookup cost is about 6% at depth 16. Round 2's flatter
  table removed one miss, but lookups plus key comparison are still 54% of rvk-1t's pipelined CPU. Batch
  prefetching was not built. It is the obvious next optimization, and the reason Valkey keeps a per-core edge
  on some pipelined points.
* **Valkey's bookkeeping.** `getMonotonicUs` at 5.0% belongs to per-command latency and statistics tracking
  that rvk does not do. That favors rvk by about 5% at depth 16 (fairness finding, REASSESSMENT.md §7).

**Attribution of the round 2 gain** (single 5 s calibration runs, `results/raw2/dev/r2-calibration.jsonl`,
not formal results): `rvk-1t` read90 at depth 16 went from 519,674 ops/s (round 1 binary) to 574,563 (review
fixes only), 593,651 (plus flattened table) and 861,485 (plus deferred writes). Deferred writes gave most of
the gain; the flattened table gave little, and it cost memory (§II.8).

* With the same allocator, round 1's rvk was within 2% of Valkey per key. The Part I comparison against a
  glibc-based Rust map (230 bytes per key) overstated the gap (REASSESSMENT.md §4.3).
* Round 2's flattened table stores 32-byte entries inline, at a load factor of 44–88%, and keeps the old table's
  pages after each resize, so small keys cost more. It bought little speed (§II.4). A product would keep the
  denser layout or add prefetching to it instead.
* **Startup** to the first PONG with an empty dataset: Valkey 37.5 ms, rvk 5.8 ms (median of 5). rvk has no data
  to load, ever; Valkey with persistence spends seconds loading (Part I: 1.6 s per million keys from RDB).
* **Failure:** with the server killed under 20k ops/s open-loop load and restarted at once, both lost all 50
  connections, so the clients saw the failure as dropped connections rather than error replies (79.4k of 200k
  operations completed in both cases). Valkey restarted in 48 ms and rvk in 25 ms, both empty, because
  persistence was off in this test. Unlike Part I's proxy, neither hides the restart from clients.

* **Multiplexing meets the pre-registered adoption bar:** 1.70× throughput and −77% server CPU per operation on
  `valkey-1t`. The other configurations show the same mechanism. Multiplexing, not a faster server, is what
  removes the per-request system calls.
* **The client-side cache does not meet it:** 1.14× throughput over multiplexing alone, and server CPU per
  client operation 9% *higher* (1.85 against 1.69 µs), although most GETs were served from the cache. The
  server's total CPU rose from about 34% to 43% of a core, so its key tracking and invalidation work cost more

  in the library's experimental cache.
* rsclient's stale-read check needs one writer per key (it compares against the newest acknowledged write). It
  reported 0 stale reads in every run without a cache.

## II.11 Limitations

Measured, but specific to this setup:

* One 4-vCPU KVM guest over loopback, with 2 CPUs for the data service. `io-threads` is outside its intended
  regime at this size. Whether rvk's lead over `io-threads` survives on 4+ server cores was **not measured**:
  the host has only 4 vCPUs, and the load generator needs 2.
* Synthetic workloads: 100-byte values, 1M keys, 50–1000 connections. No production workload exists in the
  repository.
* The load generator limits the unpipelined `rvk-2t` and two-shard points. Pipelined runs avoid that limit but
  measure a different regime.
* Round 2 is post hoc (it was designed after seeing round 1). The baselines also changed between rounds by up
  to 16% on the same host.

Not measured:

* Valkey cluster mode with real slot routing and a cluster-aware client (two client-routed shards stand in for
  it); GLIDE (redis-rs stands in for established Rust client functionality).
* Batch prefetching in rvk, TLS, persistence or replication in rvk (none exist), real networks, hours-long runs,
  and a second, independent host.
